One-Sided Verification Under Limited Expert Review

AI can now propose candidate solutions to open scientific problems far faster than experts can review them. OSVerify filters out invalid candidates while keeping the rejection rate of valid ones under a fixed budget, so expert time goes to the candidates most likely to be correct.

Abstract

AI has become increasingly helpful in scientific discovery, yet human expert review remains essential for problems without reliable automated evaluation. This becomes a fundamental challenge as candidate generation scales beyond available review capacity.

We study verification under limited human effort, where rare valid solutions need to be prioritized while invalid candidates are filtered out to reduce human workload. We adopt the one-sided verification objective that maximizes the rejection rate of invalid solutions (TNR) under a constraint on the false-rejection rate of valid ones (FNR), making human review cost an explicit term to minimize.

Drawing on the intuition that verifying a specific error can be easier than discovering one, we introduce OSVerify, an algorithmic framework that decouples error proposal from error validation. The error proposer searches for errors across different granularities by hierarchical decomposition, while the validator distinguishes substantial errors from minor or hallucinated ones.

On competition- and research-level benchmarks, OSVerify reduces the number of human reviews of invalid solutions by 46% on average compared to voting baselines under the same empirical low FNR constraint, and post-training the validator raises the reduction to 57%. As real-world use cases, we apply OSVerify to find errors in published research papers, which the original authors subsequently confirmed, and to help resolve three open conjectures in the Langlands program, confirmed by experts.

The problem

When experts are the bottleneck, a verifier should answer two questions differently: how many valid solutions does it throw away, and how much reading does it save?

Valid solutions to hard discovery problems are rare, and discarding one may lose an important result. At the same time, every invalid candidate that slips through costs an expert's time. Following the Neyman–Pearson principle, we treat these as a constrained objective rather than a single accuracy number:

maximize TNR(V) subject to FNR(V) ≤ τ TNR: fraction of invalid solutions the verifier rejects. FNR: fraction of valid solutions it wrongly rejects. Under a fixed FNR budget τ, every gain in TNR removes invalid candidates from the expert's queue.

Standard LLM-as-a-judge verification struggles under this objective. Sampling more judgments and voting helps, but saturates quickly, and at a zero FNR budget it often rejects nothing at all. What does help is changing the question: checking whether a specific, stated error invalidates a solution is far more reliable than judging the whole solution from scratch.

Method

OSVerify brings the generate-and-verify structure inside verification itself. Instead of asking “is this solution valid?”, it asks a sequence of narrower questions: “does this error hold, and does it invalidate the solution?” A single confirmed error is enough to reject, so the error becomes the natural unit of evidence.

Error proposal

The proposer recursively splits the solution into two contiguous parts, building a tree from the whole argument down to individual claims. It then inspects every node, with the full problem and solution in context, and proposes errors that could invalidate the solution.

Goal: coverage. On as many invalid solutions as possible, at least one proposed error should be real and substantial.

Error validation

Each proposed error e is checked independently against the full problem and solution. The validator first asks whether the error holds, then whether it invalidates the solution:

  • Not-Real
  • Minor
  • Substantial

Goal: precision. Hallucinated errors and minor issues should not cost a valid solution.

Decision

Sampling the validator several times estimates how likely each error is to be substantial. A solution’s rejection score is the highest such probability over its proposed errors.

OSVerify rejects when the score clears a threshold t chosen to meet the FNR budget, and passes the solution to expert review otherwise.

Learning to validate. The validator always sees the same input, a problem, a solution and one proposed error, whatever produced them. That makes it trainable across problems. We build error-level supervision (errors on valid solutions are negatives; errors on invalid solutions are positives only when a reference-assisted validator confirms them in at least 3 of 5 samples) and post-train the validator with GRPO and LoRA.

Results

We evaluate on competition-level Putnam100 and Hard2Verify (H2V), and research-level FirstProof-Eval, built from First Proof with human-labeled valid and invalid solutions. To mimic problems beyond the verifier’s own ability, we use relatively weak backbones (gpt-oss-120b and gpt-5-mini). Reference solutions and formal proof checkers are withheld from every verifier.

With gpt-oss-120b and a 5% FNR budget, OSVerify raises TNR over voting from 38.4% to 70.4% on Putnam100, from 69.2% to 84.7% on H2V, and from 35.9% to 58.0% on FirstProof-Eval. Training the validator matters most under the strictest constraint: at zero FNR it lifts Putnam100 TNR from 0% to 85.8%, and the gains carry over to FirstProof-Eval, which is never seen during training.

Both stages scale with compute. Deeper decomposition finds more substantial errors, and more validation votes per error make rejections more reliable.

Use in mathematical research

Beyond benchmarks, we apply OSVerify to the Langlands program, one of the most demanding areas of mathematics, where checking a technical argument requires scarce specialist expertise. These studies use frontier closed-source models as the verifier backbone.

Reviewing research papers

OSVerify found mathematical errors in a paper published in the Annals of Mathematics and in a recent preprint. The authors confirmed the errors and revised their papers; the main conclusions of both still hold. Every issue in the table was verified as real by human experts.

Resolving open conjectures

Used as the verifier for an agentic mathematical problem solver, with no formal proof checker anywhere in the loop, OSVerify helped produce proofs of three previously open statements in relative Langlands duality:

  1. flatness of the loop-rotated Plancherel algebra PLX,ħ over k[ħ] (Ben-Zvi–Sakellaridis–Venkatesh, Conj. 8.5.2);
  2. commutativity of PLX (Conj. 8.1.8), with (i) and (ii) in the setting of Liu–Wang, Conj. 4.36;
  3. the automorphic commutator relation (Liu–Wang, Conj. 4.45), previously known only under extra cohomological vanishing assumptions and for curves of genus other than one.

Human experts have confirmed the proofs, and the results are being prepared for submission to mathematical journals.
